Camera-scanned film · spectral calibration

Getting film colour from physics, not from taste

Scan film with a digital camera and you get a raw file whose colour depends on your light source, your sensor, and — on a negative — a heavy orange cast that turns out not to be a filter you can take back off. The usual fix is per-channel curves nudged by eye until a grey card looks grey.

This project takes the other route: model what is physically happening to the light, using the dye data manufacturers publish in their datasheets, and land on a defined measurement standard. The output is a set of .cube LUTs for DaVinci Resolve.

Narrowband light does the separation for you

A colour film holds three dyes — cyan, magenta, yellow — and each absorbs its own part of the spectrum. Scan with one white light and every camera channel sees a blend of all three, so you inherit a crosstalk problem you then have to unmix.

Scan instead with three narrow LEDs placed where each dye dominates, and the separation is done optically, before any maths. The chart below is Portra 400's actual dye data from this repo, with the three LED wavelengths marked.

The chain

From raw file to something with units

Five stages. Nothing here is hand-tuned; every cube is regenerated by a script from published data.

Scan under three LEDs

Three sequential exposures at 640, 544 and 450 nm, merged into one linear image.

Anchor the roll

Measure this particular roll's clear base and divide it out — film base density varies roll to roll, and everything downstream assumes it has been removed.

Into the density corridor

Linear values become density on a fixed 0–1 scale, so a lookup table can address them evenly.

Onto a standard

The scanner's own density becomes a published density — Status M for colour negatives, printing density for motion-picture stock, CIE XYZ for slides. This is the point of the whole exercise: a number that means the same thing on someone else's rig.

Then render it

Slides are viewed as transparencies on a D50 light table. Negatives are printed — see below.

A negative isn't a backwards photo. It's a set of printing instructions.

Colour negative film was engineered to be printed onto a particular paper. And the orange, despite the name everyone uses for it, is not a layer — there is no sheet of orange anywhere in the film.

Real image dyes absorb light they shouldn't: cyan dye soaks up some green and blue, magenta some blue. Colour negative film corrects this with coloured couplers — the chemistry that forms the magenta and cyan dyes is itself coloured, yellow and pink respectively, before it reacts. Where the image develops, the coupler is consumed and its colour goes with it. What you see as orange is the coupler that didn't react.

Which makes it self-adjusting rather than a fixed tint: wherever image dye is missing its unwanted absorption, the leftover coupler supplies exactly that much, so dye plus surviving coupler sum to a near-constant unwanted absorption at every exposure level — and a constant is what the printing step takes out. It also means the orange is a positive image, densest where the least image dye formed.

So it isn't a defect to subtract. It's half of a correction whose other half is the print. This pipeline therefore simulates the darkroom: reconstruct the dye amounts, shine a 3200 K enlarger through them, expose real RA-4 paper, develop, and view the print under D65. (The mechanism dates to W. T. Hanson's 1950 paper on colour correction with coloured couplers.)

Papers are paired like a lab pairs them

Kodak negatives print to Kodak Endura Premier; Fujifilm negatives to Fujicolor Pro Laser. Mixing them is the kind of thing that quietly looks wrong.

The paper sets the limits, not the film

How much you can actually print is a property of the paper — measured here as identical across all five stocks on each paper.

Where this currently falls down

No manufacturer publishes per-layer dye spectra for still film. They publish one combined curve. Recovering three dye curves from one combined measurement is under-determined — one spectrum cannot pin down three components — so each stock's dyes are inferred against a reference dye set.

That inference has a measurable wobble. If you swap the reference set for a plausible alternative, results move by 0.030–0.105 density. Any two stocks closer together than that cannot be told apart by this pipeline, whatever the real films do.

Works, but unmeasured

These transforms are in real use and behave as intended — but qualitatively. No figure on this page has been compared against a measurement; each one is the model reporting on itself. The open task is a validation roll: a grey-ramp exposure series, a ColorChecker frame, and colour-separation wedges read on a spectrophotometer. That is what would turn “looks right” into “agrees to within X”.

Interimage effects missing

Film layers chemically inhibit their neighbours during development, and manufacturers design this in deliberately. It is one likely reason stocks look different in ways this model cannot reproduce. It is not modelled here.

Nominal illuminants

The enlarger and viewing light are assumed rather than measured, and no print from this pipeline has been set beside a physical one.

One specific rig

Built around a Sony a7R III and a narrowband LED source. It is a research pipeline shared in case the method is useful, not a product.

Method

The datasheets are vector art, so trace the vectors

Manufacturers' charts are drawn inside the PDF as real path geometry. This project extracts those paths rather than sampling pixels off a rendered image, which gets axis calibration down to around 0.001 log exposure.

That accuracy is easy to fool, though — so tracing is only one of four steps. The one that matters most is the last: overlay the digitised result back onto the printed ink. If the curve lands on the line, then frame detection, axis origin, axis step and sampling are all correct at once.

A goodness-of-fit number proves none of that. Evenly spaced gridlines fit any origin and any step with zero residual — which is precisely how three separate axis errors slipped through before the overlay check existed. One of them had a slower film reading as more sensitive than a faster one.

The rule underneath all of it

A value nobody measured never enters a fit. Where a datasheet's data ends, the model stops — inventing a plausible tail is how you get a confidently wrong answer.

Charts on this page are drawn from the repository's own data files and shipped LUTs — the dye curves are the digitised datasheet, and the print ramps are measured through the actual cubes.

Datasheets remain the property of their manufacturers and are used here as source material.
